The Manager’s Guide

A manager is not simply the person who gives instructions. A manager is a guide who creates direction, supports people, solves problems, and helps a team succeed together.

What it means to us

Management means turning goals into shared action.

To us, “The Manager’s Guide” means knowing how to lead with fairness, confidence, and responsibility. A good manager sets clear expectations, listens to their team, makes decisions, and creates an environment where people understand their role and feel motivated to do their best.

Great managers do not control every step. They create the conditions for people to succeed.

Our leadership principle
🎯

Set clear expectations

A team works best when everyone understands the goal, the standard, and their responsibilities.

  • Explain tasks clearly.
  • Agree on deadlines and priorities.
  • Make success easy to recognise.
🤝

Build trust and respect

People are more likely to contribute when they feel heard, valued, and treated fairly.

  • Listen before judging.
  • Give honest feedback kindly.
  • Recognise effort and improvement.
📈

Support progress

A manager should help the team improve, not just point out what is wrong.

  • Check in regularly.
  • Solve problems early.
  • Encourage learning and confidence.

How to achieve it

Four habits of an effective manager

These habits help managers lead in a practical and positive way. They turn expectations into everyday actions.

01 Communicate clearly and regularly
02 Lead by example, not just by instruction
03 Stay organised and make fair decisions
04 Reflect, improve, and help others grow

Personal Reflections on Technology and Cybersecurity

Perspectives on the benefits, risks, and vulnerabilities of modern technology.

Blog Post 01 - Julius

My Opinion on Technology and Its Vulnerabilities

Today’s technology is powerful, useful, and almost impossible to avoid. I use my phone and laptop every day for communication, studying, entertainment, shopping, and storing personal information. Because of this, I believe it is important to acknowledge the vulnerabilities that come with modern devices instead of pretending they do not exist.

Technology has made life easier in many ways. Smartphones allow us to connect with people instantly, laptops help us complete school and work tasks, and online services make everyday activities more convenient. However, the same devices that help us can also expose us to risks such as hacking, phishing, malware, identity theft, and data leaks. These vulnerabilities are not always obvious, but they can have serious effects if users are careless.

In my opinion, ignoring technology vulnerabilities is dangerous. Some people assume that cyber threats only happen to companies or people with a lot of money, but anyone can become a target. A weak password, an unsafe Wi-Fi connection, or a suspicious link can put personal information at risk. This is why users need to be aware of how their devices work and how attackers may try to take advantage of them.

When I use devices like my mobile phone and laptop, I try to acknowledge these risks and take basic precautions. For example, I believe it is important to use strong passwords, enable two-factor authentication, update software regularly, avoid downloading unknown files, and be careful with links or messages that seem suspicious. These steps may seem small, but they can help protect personal data and reduce the chance of cyber attacks.

I also think that companies and technology developers have a responsibility to make devices and applications safer. Users should be informed about privacy settings, security updates, and possible risks. At the same time, users should not depend completely on companies to protect them. Cybersecurity is a shared responsibility between developers, organizations, and everyday users.

Overall, I believe modern technology is extremely valuable, but it should be used with awareness. I do not think we should fear technology, but we should respect the risks that come with it. By acknowledging vulnerabilities and practicing safe habits, we can enjoy the benefits of phones, laptops, and online services while protecting ourselves from avoidable threats.

Blog Post 02 - Khaldon

The Benefits and Risks of Constantly Improving Technology

Technology is constantly changing, and I believe this brings both exciting benefits and serious risks. One of the biggest strengths of improving technology is that it makes everyday life faster, easier, and more convenient. Smartphones, online banking, artificial intelligence, and cloud storage help people communicate, study, work, and solve problems more efficiently than ever before. Technology also gives people better access to information and creates new opportunities for businesses, education, healthcare, and transport.

However, these improvements also come with weaknesses. Many people become too dependent on their devices and may lose practical skills because technology does so much for them. Constant updates can also be stressful, especially for people who struggle to keep up with new systems. At the same time, technology creates risks such as cybercrime, job loss through automation, privacy problems, and the spread of false information online.

The aspect I want to focus on most is privacy. As technology improves, more personal data is collected by apps, websites, and smart devices. They can track what we search for, where we go, what we buy, and what we are interested in. This can make services more personalised and useful, but it can also be dangerous if the data is stolen, sold, or misused. In my opinion, improving technology is a positive thing, but only when people understand the risks. We should not reject new technology, but we should use it carefully. Strong passwords, privacy settings, regular updates, and digital awareness are important because technology should support people, not control them.

Blog Post 03 - Christian

My Opinion on Technology and Its Vulnerabilities

Technology is always evolving, and I think that is both exciting and worrying. The biggest benefit is that new technology can make life easier, faster, and more connected. It helps people communicate across the world, improves healthcare, supports education, and allows businesses to work more efficiently. For example, artificial intelligence can help doctors detect illnesses earlier, while online learning gives more people access to information. These improvements show how technology can solve real problems and create new opportunities for people who may not have had them before.

However, constant improvement also creates weaknesses. As technology becomes more advanced, people and organizations often depend on it too much. If systems fail, are hacked, or are used irresponsibly, the damage can be serious. Personal data can be stolen, jobs can change or disappear, and people may lose important skills because machines do more of the thinking for them. The same tools that make life convenient can also make people vulnerable if they are not managed carefully.

The aspect I find most important is cybersecurity. As technology grows, more personal information is stored online, including passwords, bank details, medical records, and private messages. This creates a major opportunity to protect people through stronger security systems, better encryption, and smarter ways to detect cyberattacks. At the same time, it creates a threat because criminals also use evolving technology to become more advanced. In my opinion, technology should keep improving, but safety must improve with it. Progress is valuable only when people can trust the systems they use every day. Without that trust, even the most impressive technology can become a risk instead of a benefit.

Legal or Illegal? Use of AI in Academia

Three academic scenarios showing acceptable, questionable, and unacceptable uses of AI.

Blog Post 04 - AI in Academia

Case 1: Using AI to Improve an Essay Draft

A university student writes an essay about climate change and then uses an AI tool to check grammar, improve sentence structure, and make the writing clearer. The student does not ask the AI to create the essay from scratch. Instead, they use it like a writing assistant after completing their own research, planning, and first draft.

Reveal category
GREEN: Acceptable use that supports learning

This can support learning because the student still develops their own ideas and arguments. The AI helps with language improvement, but the academic work remains the student’s own. The student should still review every suggestion carefully and follow the university’s AI policy.

What does green mean: This use supports language improvement and does not replace the student’s own thinking or research.

Blog Post 05 - AI in Academia

Case 2: Using AI to Summarize Academic Articles

A student is preparing for a seminar and uses AI to summarize three long academic articles. The summaries help the student understand the main ideas more quickly before reading the full texts. However, the student does not rely only on the AI summary and still checks the original sources for important arguments, evidence, and quotations.

Reveal category
YELLOW: Requires careful consideration

This situation can be useful, but it needs careful handling. AI summaries can miss details, misunderstand arguments, or simplify complex academic ideas too much. The student should ask the professor whether this kind of AI use is allowed and should never cite the AI summary as if it were the original article.

What does yellow mean: This may be useful with proper guidelines, but it could risk learning objectives if the student avoids thinking by himself.

Blog Post 06 - AI in Academia

Case 3: Submitting an AI-Written Assignment

A student has a deadline for a history assignment and asks an AI tool to write the full essay. The student copies the AI-generated text, makes only a few small changes, and submits it under their own name without telling the teacher. The student also includes sources suggested by AI without checking whether they are real or accurate.

Reveal category
RED: Unacceptable use

This is a serious academic problem because the submitted work does not represent the student’s own knowledge, effort, or understanding. It also prevents the teacher from properly assessing the student’s skills. In many schools and universities, this would be treated as academic misconduct.

What does red mean: This violates academic integrity, circumvents the learning process, and is likely prohibited by academic policy.

Discussion: AI, Learning, and Critical Thinking

Reflections on how AI can support education while still protecting independent thinking, fairness, and academic challenge.

Blog Post 07 - Discussion Question 1

Supporting Learning Without Letting AI Think for Us

The line between using AI to support learning and relying on it too much should be drawn at the point where the student stops making their own decisions. AI can be helpful when it explains difficult ideas, gives feedback on a draft, suggests questions to consider, or helps organize information. In these cases, the learner is still responsible for understanding the topic and choosing what to write or believe.

However, AI becomes a problem when it replaces the student’s thinking. If a student asks AI to produce a full answer and submits it without understanding it, the learning process has been skipped. The student may get a finished product, but they have not built the skills that the assignment was designed to develop.

In our opinion, AI should be treated like a tutor, not a substitute brain. A good tutor asks questions, explains concepts, and helps students improve. It does not take the test for them. Students should use AI to become better thinkers, not to avoid thinking altogether.

Blog Post 08 - Discussion Question 2

Productive Resistance in AI Learning Tools

Productive resistance means making AI tools helpful without making them too easy. Instead of giving students complete answers immediately, AI could ask them to explain what they already know, show their first attempt, or choose between possible solutions. This would make the student participate actively instead of only copying a response.

Another useful form of resistance would be requiring evidence. For example, if a student asks for help with an argument, the AI could ask them to provide a source or could remind them to compare the answer with class materials. The AI could also give hints before full solutions, similar to how a teacher might guide a student step by step.

The challenge is that the resistance should not feel like punishment. If AI tools become too frustrating, students may stop using them or find easier tools elsewhere. The best approach is balanced: AI should support progress, but it should also slow students down just enough to make them think.

Blog Post 09 - Discussion Question 3

Redesigning Assignments for an AI World

Teachers can redesign assignments by focusing more on the learning process instead of only the final product. If an assignment only asks for a polished essay, students may be tempted to let AI do most of the work. But if the assignment includes planning notes, source evaluation, drafts, reflections, and explanations of choices, it becomes harder for AI to replace the student’s thinking.

One useful method is to ask students to show how they used AI. For example, students could include a short AI-use statement explaining what tool they used, what prompts they entered, what suggestions they accepted, and what they rejected. This makes AI use more transparent and encourages students to think critically about the output.

Teachers could also create assignments that connect to personal experience, class discussions, local issues, or oral presentations. These tasks require students to apply ideas, defend their reasoning, and respond to questions. AI can still assist with preparation, but it cannot fully replace the student’s understanding.

Blog Post 10 - Discussion Question 4

Rules for AI Access in Schools and Exams

Governments and schools should play an important role in regulating AI access, especially during exams. Schools need clear rules so students understand when AI is allowed, when it must be cited, and when it is completely prohibited. Without clear rules, students may be confused about what counts as help and what counts as cheating.

During exams, AI access should usually be restricted unless the exam is specifically designed to test how students use AI responsibly. Traditional exams often measure memory, reasoning, writing, and problem-solving under controlled conditions. If some students can secretly use AI, the assessment becomes unfair.

Governments can help by creating broad standards for privacy, safety, and fairness, while schools can create detailed classroom policies. The goal should not be to ban AI completely, because AI is already part of modern life. Instead, the goal should be responsible access: students should learn how to use AI ethically, but exams should still protect honest assessment.

Blog Post 11 - Discussion Question 5

Personalised Learning Should Be Based on Evidence, Not Just AI Memory

If AI is used to personalise learning, it should not only rely on what the model already “knows” from training. Large language models can be useful, but they can also make mistakes, invent information, or give answers that sound correct without showing where the information came from. For personalised learning to be trustworthy, the AI should be connected to reliable datasets, school-approved materials, academic databases, and trusted websites.

A technical way to do this is called Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG. With RAG, the AI does not simply answer from memory. Instead, it first searches through a controlled knowledge base, such as textbooks, teacher notes, research articles, lesson materials, or verified educational websites. Then it uses the retrieved information to create an answer. This makes the learning process more evidence-based because the response is connected to real sources rather than only generated from the model itself.

To make this work properly, schools should use trusted source collections. For example, an AI learning system could search only in approved datasets, official curriculum documents, digital libraries, peer-reviewed articles, and websites from universities, governments, or recognised educational organisations. The AI should show citations or links for the information it uses, so students and teachers can check whether the answer is accurate. This also helps students learn how to verify information instead of accepting AI responses blindly.

Personalisation should also include intellectual challenge. If an AI only gives easier explanations or direct answers, students may become too dependent on it. A better system would adapt to the learner’s level but still ask follow-up questions, request evidence, compare different sources, and explain why an answer is correct. For example, instead of only giving a final answer, the AI could ask the student to justify their reasoning, identify the source that supports their claim, or compare two possible interpretations.

In our opinion, personalised AI learning should be designed like a guided research assistant, not like an answer machine. RAG, trusted datasets, source checking, and teacher-approved materials can make AI more reliable and educational. This way, students still benefit from personal support, but their learning is based on evidence, sources, and critical thinking rather than depending completely on AI-generated knowledge.

Opinions: Digital ID and AI - What Does the Future Hold?

Opinion pieces on what the future of digital ID might look like, especially as AI changes identity verification, privacy, and security.

Blog Post 12 - Julius

What Technology Will Replace Today’s Authentication Methods by 2040?

By 2040, I think today’s authentication methods, especially passwords and even basic two-factor authentication, will mostly be replaced by biometric and behavior-based identity systems. Passwords have been useful for decades, but they are also one of the weakest parts of digital security. People reuse them, forget them, write them down, or fall for phishing attacks. Even when companies require stronger passwords, users often find ways to make them easier to remember, which usually makes them easier to guess or steal.

In my opinion, the future of authentication will be based on who we are and how we behave, rather than what we remember. Biometric tools like facial recognition, fingerprints, voice recognition, and iris scans will likely become more advanced and more common. However, I do not think biometrics alone will be enough. A stronger system will combine biometrics with behavioral authentication, such as how a person types, walks, holds a phone, or moves a mouse. These patterns are difficult for hackers to copy because they happen naturally and continuously.

I also believe authentication will become more invisible. Instead of logging in with a password every time, devices and systems may constantly verify users in the background. For example, a phone or computer could confirm that the right person is using it based on face, voice, location, and behavior. If something seems unusual, the system could ask for an extra check. This would make security stronger while also making the user experience smoother.

Still, this future raises serious privacy concerns. If companies collect more biometric and behavioral data, they must protect it carefully. Unlike a password, a person cannot easily change their face, fingerprint, or voice if that information is stolen. Because of this, I think authentication by 2040 will need to include strong privacy protections, encryption, and user control over personal data.

Overall, I believe passwords will not completely disappear, but they will no longer be the main way people prove their identity. By 2040, authentication will likely be more personal, automatic, and biometric. The best replacement for today’s methods will be a system that is secure, convenient, and respectful of privacy.
